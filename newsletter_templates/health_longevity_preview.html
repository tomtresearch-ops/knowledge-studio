Your NAD+ Supplements Might Be Missing the Point
Plus: why living longer and living well aren't the same thing, and a GLP-1 surprise that has nothing to do with weight loss.
February 17, 2026 · 3 min read · Listen to this issue
Three things caught my attention this week in longevity research. One of them might change how you think about a supplement you're already taking. Another challenges a fundamental assumption the entire field has been operating on. And the third is a drug millions of people are already prescribed — that might have a completely different benefit nobody was looking for.
01
NAD+ supplementation might be solving the wrong problem
If you're taking NMN or NR to boost your NAD+ levels, new research suggests you might be measuring the wrong thing. A study published in FASEB Journal found that it's the ratio of NAD+ to NADH — not the absolute amount of NAD+ — that determines which metabolic pathways activate in your cells. Reduced and oxidized NAD+ precursors trigger entirely different outcomes. The implication is significant: simply flooding your system with more NAD+ precursors without addressing the redox balance may explain why many people don't see the benefits they expect.
What to watch for: reformulated NAD+ protocols that pair NMN/NR with electron transport chain modulators like ubiquinol or carnitine derivatives. The goal shifts from "more NAD+" to "better NAD+ balance."
02
Living longer and living well are not the same biological process
This one matters more than it sounds. A meta-analysis in Aging and Disease found that the interventions that extend maximum lifespan in mice — caloric restriction, rapamycin — don't reliably improve functional healthspan. The biological pathways for "more years" and "better years" are mechanistically different. That's a problem, because most longevity research has been optimizing for the first one and assuming the second comes along for the ride. It doesn't.
The bottom line: if your longevity protocol is based on mouse lifespan studies, ask whether the same interventions have been shown to preserve mobility, cognition, and independence — not just delay death.
03
GLP-1 drugs might protect your brain — not just your waistline
Semaglutide and tirzepatide are everywhere right now for weight loss. But a review in the Journal of Clinical Investigation is pulling together converging evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists also reduce neuroinflammation and may slow the progression of Parkinson's disease. Phase 2b human trials are expected by mid-2026. If they validate what the rodent and early human data suggest, expect a wave of off-label use for cognitive preservation in aging — completely independent of diabetes or weight management.
Worth tracking: the "emotional flatness" some GLP-1 users report may actually reflect dopamine circuit modulation — which is mechanistically interesting and relevant to the neuroprotection story.
The Bigger Picture
A pattern is emerging across this week's research: the aging field is moving from simple interventions to understanding that biological systems have balance points, not just levels to optimize. NAD+ redox ratios matter more than NAD+ quantity. Healthspan and lifespan diverge mechanistically. GLP-1 drugs affect circuits beyond metabolism. The next generation of longevity protocols will look less like "take this supplement" and more like "rebalance this system." That's harder — but it's also more likely to work.
