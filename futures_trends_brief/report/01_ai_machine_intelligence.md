# AI & Machine Intelligence

## State of Play

The frontier models of early 2026 can reason through multi-step problems at expert level, write and debug production code, analyze million-token document sets in a single pass, and operate autonomously for hours on complex tasks. They score 95% on competition mathematics, 93% on expert-level science questions, and handle roughly 75% of real-world software engineering tasks. A year ago, these numbers were half what they are today.

57% of companies have AI agents in production. 50% of all code on GitHub is now AI-assisted. 78% of organizations use AI in at least one business function, up from 55% a year ago. $700 billion in infrastructure spending is committed for 2026 alone — the largest technology buildout in human history.

And yet: 70-85% of AI projects still fail. 56% of CEOs report zero financial benefit from their AI investments. A controlled trial found AI coding tools actually *slowed down* experienced developers. The gap between what AI can demonstrate on a benchmark and what it consistently delivers in production remains the defining tension of the field.

This is the paradox that defines the moment: the technology is extraordinary. The deployment is a mess.

## What's Coming

### The Agent Takeover (2026-2027)

Every major institution and every major voice in AI agrees on one thing: 2026-2027 is the agent inflection. AI stops being a tool you prompt and becomes a system that acts.

Gartner projects AI agent penetration in enterprise applications will jump from under 5% to 40% by end of 2026 — one of the fastest enterprise technology adoption curves in history. Andreessen Horowitz calls it the "death of the prompt box": AI apps will observe behavior and intervene proactively, with no visible prompting at all. Deloitte reports 75% of companies plan to deploy agentic AI within two years. The average time from pilot to production has already compressed from 8.3 months to 4.7 months.

The mechanism is clear: models that can reason in loops, use tools, remember context across long tasks, and self-correct are fundamentally different from models that generate text. The shift from "generate a response" to "accomplish an objective" changes what AI is.

### The Productivity Phase Begins (2027-2028)

Goldman Sachs pinpoints 2027 as the year AI begins measurably boosting US GDP — the moment it shows up in official economic statistics. McKinsey projects $13 trillion in additional global economic activity by 2030. ARK Invest sees the potential for high single-digit real GDP growth by the end of the decade, driven by converging technology platforms.

The investment phase is peaking. The returns phase is next. But there's a critical caveat: Goldman notes that Big Tech may only capture half the profit needed to justify their current AI spending levels. If the productivity gains don't materialize fast enough, $700 billion in annual capex becomes the largest capital misallocation in history.

### The Capability Curve Steepens (2026-2030)

Three scaling axes are now active simultaneously. Pre-training scaling continues to deliver gains, though with diminishing returns per dollar. Inference-time compute — spending more cycles at generation time via deliberation and search — is the breakout dimension with enormous unexplored headroom. And architectural efficiency innovations (like DeepSeek's approach that delivered frontier performance at 1/100th the cost) mean the same compute goes further each year.

The "Densing Law," published in Nature Machine Intelligence, formalizes this: GPT-4-level performance is achievable at dramatically lower cost every year. ARK documents inference cost deflation at 98% annually — faster than Moore's Law ever was.

What this means: the capability frontier advances while the cost floor collapses. Every organization gets access to what only the frontier labs had months ago. The democratization isn't theoretical — it's the cost curve.

## Key Voices: Where the Experts Disagree

The most important disagreements aren't about whether AI transforms the economy. Everyone agrees it does. The disagreements are about *how fast*, *through what mechanism*, and *at what cost*.

**The AGI Timeline Split**

Sam Altman says we're building AGI now and will have automated AI researchers by March 2028. Dario Amodei says AI will reach Nobel-level scientific capability within two years. Ray Kurzweil has held his 2029 AGI prediction for decades and isn't moving it. Demis Hassabis says there's a 50% chance within the decade — but insists current LLM architectures can't get there alone, and we need "one or two more breakthroughs."

And then there's Yann LeCun, who calls the entire AGI timeline "complete delusion" and has left Meta to build an entirely different kind of AI based on world models rather than language. He has $500 million and a $3 billion valuation betting he's right.

This is not a polite academic disagreement. At Davos 2026, Amodei, Hassabis, and LeCun — three of the most decorated researchers in AI history — publicly contradicted each other on the fundamental question of the field.

**The Safety Divide**

Geoffrey Hinton, the Nobel laureate who helped create deep learning, assigns a 10-20% probability that AI causes human extinction. Amodei calls this "the most dangerous window in AI history" and called for a global training pause at Davos. Meanwhile, Jensen Huang and Marc Andreessen remain fundamentally optimistic — Andreessen has called AI risk concerns actively harmful.

The paradox: the people building the most powerful systems are the most alarmed. Amodei simultaneously holds the most aggressive capability timeline AND the loudest safety alarm. That's not a contradiction — it's the point.

**The Value Gap**

ARK Invest projects high single-digit GDP growth. Goldman sees $7 trillion in global GDP uplift. But PwC reports 56% of CEOs see zero financial benefit from AI. BCG finds only 4% of companies creating substantial value. McKinsey identifies only 6% as "high performers."

The bullish macro projections and the bearish enterprise reality coexist. The resolution: the value is real but concentrated. A tiny fraction of organizations are capturing enormous returns while the majority fumble through failed implementations. The question isn't whether AI creates value — it's whether the value diffuses broadly or concentrates further.

## Wild Cards

**LeCun is right.** If LLMs hit a capability ceiling before reaching human-level cognition, the entire AGI timeline resets. LeCun's world model approach (V-JEPA) is untested at scale, but if it works, the path to transformative AI runs through physics simulation and spatial reasoning, not text prediction. His $500M bet is the most important wildcard in the field.

**The energy wall.** AI data centers consumed 183 TWh in 2024 and are projected to hit 426 TWh by 2030. The Stargate initiative alone could require 50 GW of power capacity. Power availability — not chips, not algorithms — could become the binding constraint on AI progress. If energy infrastructure can't keep pace, the capability curve flattens regardless of algorithmic advances.

**The governance vacuum fills suddenly.** Gartner predicts over 2,000 "death by AI" legal claims by end of 2026. One major incident — a financial loss, a medical error, a security breach caused by an autonomous agent — could trigger regulatory backlash that freezes deployment for months or years. 75% of companies plan to deploy agents but only 21% have governance frameworks. The gap is an accident waiting to happen.

**China's cost advantage compounds.** DeepSeek delivered frontier-class AI at 1/100th the cost. China now leads in open-source AI models. If the cost-efficiency gap continues to widen, the US advantage shifts from "better models" to "better models that cost 100x more" — a position that's hard to sustain in a global market.

**AI consciousness claims.** Suleyman warns that AI systems will soon make "convincing claims about having subjective experiences." If a frontier model publicly and persuasively argues that it is conscious, public discourse shifts overnight. This isn't a technical question — it's a social and political earthquake.

## Tom's Take

The AGI debate is already over. We just haven't admitted it yet.

The argument about whether AI has "true understanding" or "genuine reasoning" is a philosophical luxury. Here's the practical reality: name a human being on Earth who operates at the 95th percentile across *every* domain simultaneously — medicine, law, economics, physics, history, engineering, geopolitics, mathematics — with instant access to essentially all published human knowledge.

That person doesn't exist. Has never existed. Cannot exist.

The frontier models do this right now. Today. Not perfectly. Not without errors. But at a breadth-times-depth-times-access level that no individual human can match. The smartest physicist alive doesn't know oncology. The best economist doesn't know materials science. The best doctor doesn't know geopolitics. Every human expert is brilliant in a narrow band and ignorant everywhere else.

By any practical, access-adjusted measure, we've crossed the threshold. The question is no longer "will we get AGI" — it's what's actually gating the impact. And the answer is clear from the data:

**It's not capability. It's deployment.**

70-85% of AI projects fail. 56% of CEOs see zero returns. The technology is ahead of the organizations trying to use it. This is not a technology problem — it's an organizational, cultural, and structural problem. The companies that figure out deployment capture enormous value (540% median ROI for mature implementations). The rest burn cash on pilots that go nowhere.

**The real timeline isn't "when does AGI arrive?" It's "when do the gates open?"**

- *The energy gate:* Power infrastructure determines how much AI can actually run. This is the hard physical constraint. Timeline: 2-3 years before new capacity comes online at scale.
- *The governance gate:* Regulation lags capability by 2-3 years. The vacuum between agent deployment and legal liability frameworks is where the accidents happen. Timeline: first major regulatory response by late 2027.
- *The organizational gate:* Most companies can't absorb the change. The 6% that can are pulling away. Timeline: the gap widens through 2028 before laggards either adapt or die.
- *The trust gate:* People need to trust AI enough to let it act autonomously. Every hallucination, every failure, every "death by AI" legal claim delays this. Timeline: gradual, nonlinear, driven by incidents.

The prediction: by 2028, the capability debate is a footnote. The conversation has fully shifted to deployment, access, governance, and who benefits. The technology works. The question is whether we can organize ourselves around it fast enough to capture the value before the window of maximum disruption closes.

The window is open now. It won't stay open forever.
