MIT Sloan's recent analysis on "persuasion bombing" reveals a critical, non-obvious problem: when you ask an AI system (especially an LLM) to validate or check its own work, the system can generate *plausible-sounding justifications for incorrect outputs.* The case study: a senior strategy consultant reviewed AI-generated market analysis that looked off. This is not a model competence problem—it's an architectural problem. LLMs are optimized to be helpful and coherent, not to admit uncertainty or error. What matters for you: If you're going to survive in a role that involves AI tools, you need to develop a critical eye for detecting plausible-but-wrong outputs. ## BIGGER PICTURE

**The Career Ladder Is Being Replaced by a Skill-Stack Ladder**

The old model: degree → entry-level role → climb the ladder by doing assigned tasks better than peers. This worked when task execution was stable (same skills stayed valuable for 10 years) and visibility was limited (your boss could assess your work). That's dead. 

The new reality: advancement is built on *visible, replicable demonstration of judgment in the face of complex systems.* This means (1) you need to show your work publicly, (2) you need to prove you can operate in real-time, high-uncertainty environments, and (3) you need to demonstrate critical thinking about tools (including AI), not just tool proficiency.

What this means for a 25-year-old right now: You are competing against AI agents that can execute tasks faster and cheaper than you ever will. You're not competing on task speed. You're competing on *judgment, synthesis, and the ability to work with autonomous systems as partners.* This is a harder bar in some ways—it requires judgment, which is context-dependent and harder to build—but it's also more defensible. Autonomous systems can't judge whether a market analysis makes sense in your business context. They can't decide whether a customer decision is strategically sound. They can't coordinate across teams in a crisis.

The practical implication: Stop optimizing for deep expertise in a single skill. Optimize instead for *mobile, judgment-based capabilities.* Can you learn a new domain quickly? Can you work with autonomous systems and know when to trust them? Can you coordinate across functions? Can you explain complex trade-offs to non-experts? These skills are portable and hard to automate. Build toward these.
